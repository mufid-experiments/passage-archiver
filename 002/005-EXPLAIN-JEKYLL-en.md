### Is it valid Japanese?

{% include explain/verdict.html n="005" %}

The line is grammatical, but it does not end a sentence. It holds two clauses, each with its own topic. In the first, <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>は “the spring breeze” is the topic, and <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り, the continuative form (<ruby>連<rt>れん</rt>用<rt>よう</rt>形<rt>けい</rt></ruby>) of the verb <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>る “be fragrant”, ends the clause and links it to the next (<ruby>連<rt>れん</rt>用<rt>よう</rt>中<rt>ちゅう</rt>止<rt>し</rt>法<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>, a written, slightly literary way of saying “…and”). In the second, やがて “before long” introduces <ruby>緑<rt>みどり</rt></ruby>は “the green”, and <ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>いて, the te-form of <ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>く “bud, sprout”, leaves it open. The sentence continues to line 006, where a third clause with a third topic (<ruby>桜<rt>さくら</rt></ruby>は) ends it on the terminal <ruby>散<rt>ち</rt></ruby>ってゆく. The unusual part is <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>る: a colour word describing a scent. That is a poetic choice rather than an error (see Notes). The space marks the break between the two clauses, where prose would put a comma. Line 004 closes its own sentence with のかな, so this line, after the stanza break, starts the refrain with a new sentence. Restored prose: <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り、やがて<ruby>緑<rt>みどり</rt></ruby>は<ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>いて、<ruby>祝<rt>しゅく</rt>福<rt>ふく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>光<rt>ひかり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>、また<ruby>桜<rt>さくら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>散<rt>ち</rt></ruby>ってゆく。

### How it fits together

{% include explain/tree.html n="005" %}

The sentence of lines 005–006 has three clauses, each with its own は-topic, in the order of a passing spring: breeze, buds, falling blossoms. <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り (<ruby>連<rt>れん</rt>用<rt>よう</rt>中<rt>ちゅう</rt>止<rt>し</rt></ruby>) and <ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>いて (te-form) both mean “…and”; neither has a tense of its own, and both take the non-past of ゆく on line 006. やがて belongs to the second clause and marks time moving on from the first.

### In Tae Kim’s guide

{% include explain/taekim.html n="005" %}

6.1.4 is the stem in place of the te-form: “formal speeches, narration, and written publications employ the verb stem instead of the te-form to describe sequential actions”, as in <ruby>企<rt>き</rt>業<rt>ぎょう</rt>内<rt>ない</rt></ruby>の<ruby>顧<rt>こ</rt>客<rt>きゃく</rt></ruby>データを<ruby>利<rt>り</rt>用<rt>よう</rt></ruby>し、… “using the company’s internal customer data, …”. 4.1.2 mentions the same use of the stem in formal writing. The line sets this written <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り beside the spoken te-form <ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>いて (4.4.2). 3.12.1 turns an i-adjective into an adverb with く (<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く), which gives <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く. やがて is vocabulary, and a colour word for a scent is a matter of style that the book does not take up.

## Notes

### <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>る

Japanese <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>い covers much of what English splits into blue and green (<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt>信<rt>しん</rt>号<rt>ごう</rt></ruby> “green light”, <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt>葉<rt>ば</rt></ruby> “green leaves”, <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>りんご “green apple”). It also means “fresh, young, unripe” (<ruby>青<rt>せい</rt>春<rt>しゅん</rt></ruby> “youth”, <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt>二<rt>に</rt>才<rt>さい</rt></ruby> “greenhorn”). So <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>る suggests a scent of new greenery and of youth at once. The pattern is ordinary, an adverbial 〜く before a verb of smelling (<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>く<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>る “smell sweet”), but a colour word in that slot is a poet’s choice: a scent described as if it could be seen. Haiku has the related season word <ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt>薫<rt>かお</rt></ruby>る (かぜかおる), the fresh breeze of early summer, written with <ruby>薫<rt>かお</rt></ruby>, another kanji for かおる.

### Another parse

An adjective’s continuative form can also end a clause on its own (<ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>い “the sky is blue and the sea is wide”). Read that way, <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く、<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り would mean “the spring breeze is green and fragrant”. The lyric writes <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り without a space, which favours the first reading, and the meaning hardly changes.

### <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り: verb, not noun

<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り is the <ruby>連<rt>れん</rt>用<rt>よう</rt>形<rt>けい</rt></ruby> of <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>る and also the noun “fragrance”. The noun would need a different frame (<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>い<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り（だ） “the spring breeze is a green scent”, or <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り “the scent of the spring breeze”). Here <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り has a subject (<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>は) and an adverb (<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>く), so it is the verb. The <ruby>連<rt>れん</rt>用<rt>よう</rt>中<rt>ちゅう</rt>止<rt>し</rt>法<rt>ほう</rt></ruby> sounds more written than the te-form <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>って, and the line has both kinds of link in a row: <ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り, then <ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>いて.

### Three topics with は

<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>は, <ruby>緑<rt>みどり</rt></ruby>は and <ruby>桜<rt>さくら</rt></ruby>は (line 006) give each element of the scene its own clause and set them side by side, like frames of a picture: the breeze…, the green…, the cherry blossoms…. With が (<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り、<ruby>緑<rt>みどり</rt></ruby>が<ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>いて…) the lines would read more like a report of events. The parallel は also fits a cycle that comes round every year, which line 006’s また “again” spells out.

### やがて

Modern やがて means “before long, in due course”, time passing naturally (classical Japanese also used it for “immediately”). The song uses it once more, on line 016 (やがて<ruby>景<rt>け</rt>色<rt>しき</rt></ruby>は<ruby>流<rt>なが</rt></ruby>れてゆく), again for things outside the speaker that move on by themselves.

### Link to line 004

Line 004 (<ruby>言<rt>こと</rt>葉<rt>ば</rt></ruby>にして <ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えられるのかな) ends the first stanza on a question the speaker asks themselves. Nothing carries over in grammar. The refrain answers the doubt with a picture of spring instead of an argument. Lines 005–006 return word for word as lines 039–040, after the song has answered the doubt on line 036 (<ruby>言<rt>こと</rt>葉<rt>ば</rt></ruby>にしてきたんだ).

### Link to line 006

<ruby>芽<rt>め</rt>吹<rt>ぶ</rt></ruby>いて leaves the sentence open, and line 006 closes it: <ruby>祝<rt>しゅく</rt>福<rt>ふく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>光<rt>ひかり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> また<ruby>桜<rt>さくら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>散<rt>ち</rt></ruby>ってゆく. In a Japanese spring the new leaves and the falling cherry blossoms overlap; the refrain puts the falling blossoms last, so the scene ends on loss inside celebration.
